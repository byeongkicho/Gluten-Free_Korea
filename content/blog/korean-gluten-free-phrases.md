*First published August 2026. The Korean here is written by a Korean speaker in Korea, not run through a translator — that is the one thing we can offer that a translation app cannot.*

If you only take one sentence to Korea, take this one:

> **제가 밀가루 알러지가 있어요. 이거 밀가루 들어가나요?**
> "I have a wheat flour allergy. Does this contain wheat flour?"

Copy it with the button and keep it somewhere you can pull up fast. That sentence, held up on a phone screen, has done more work for us than any amount of explaining.

**Don't try to pronounce it.** You'll notice there is no pronunciation guide anywhere in this post, and that's deliberate: romanised Korean is hard to say, and staff frequently don't recognise it when a traveller attempts it. Showing works. Saying usually doesn't. Every Korean line here has a copy button for exactly that reason.

The rest of this post is the phrases that come *after* the opener — the reconfirmation, the hidden-sauce questions, the grill-plate ask — because in our experience the first answer you get is often not the real one.

## Why "allergy" beats "gluten-free" in Korea

The literal translation of gluten-free is **글루텐프리**. It is a real word here. It is also the wrong tool.

In our experience "gluten" reaches most restaurant staff as a *preference* — the same mental category as low-carb. We can't cite a prevalence figure for coeliac disease in Korea and won't invent one; what we can report is that across a year of asking, we did not meet a single server or bakery owner who treated 글루텐프리 as a medical constraint until we changed the word. **밀가루** (wheat flour) and **알러지** (allergy) land in a completely different category: a medical constraint the kitchen is responsible for. Frame it as an allergy and someone actually walks back to ask the cook.

We want to be precise about what that claim is. It comes from our own eating between 2025 and 2026, as a couple with one gluten-free person, across Seoul, Gyeonggi and Cheonan — the places we've been to are listed with notes in [our directory](/places). We did not count conversations or record outcomes, so this is a consistent impression, not a measured rate. Read it as the pattern we now act on rather than as evidence you could audit.

**알러지 or 알레르기?** Both work. 알레르기 is the standard spelling and the one you'll meet on packaging — the allergen line on Korean labels reads **알레르기 유발물질**. 알러지 is the everyday spoken form. Since you'll be showing the text rather than saying it, the choice costs you nothing either way.

## The phrases, by situation

### 1. Opening the conversation

| Korean | English |
|---|---|
| 제가 밀가루 알러지가 있어요. | I have a wheat flour allergy. |
| 이거 밀가루 들어가나요? | Does this contain wheat flour? |
| 밀가루 없는 메뉴 있나요? | Do you have anything without wheat flour? |

### 2. Reconfirming — the part people skip

The first answer is frequently a breezy **"안 들어가요~"** ("nope, doesn't have it") delivered without anyone leaving the counter. That is the moment to push, politely:

| Korean | English |
|---|---|
| 죄송한데 주방에 한 번만 확인해 주실 수 있을까요? | Sorry — could you check with the kitchen just once? |
| 알러지가 심해서요. 조금만 들어가도 안 돼요. | My allergy is severe. Even a little is a problem. |

Korean service culture is accommodating, and a specific, apologetic ask is usually met well. The goal isn't to doubt the server; it's to move the question to the person who knows.

### 3. Naming the hidden sources

This is where most of the value is, and it is not optional — because the opening phrase has a hole in it.

**밀가루 means wheat *flour* specifically.** Brewed soy sauce is fermented with wheat the grain, not with flour. So a kitchen can answer "밀가루는 안 들어가요" — no wheat flour — **completely honestly** about a soy-braised dish that still contains wheat. Nobody is lying to you; you asked a narrower question than you meant.

That is on top of the ordinary problem: **간장** (soy sauce), **고추장** (gochujang) and **된장** (doenjang) are filed mentally as seasoning, not as ingredients. Ask about them by name:

| Korean | English |
|---|---|
| 간장 들어가나요? | Does it contain soy sauce? |
| 양념에 고추장 들어가나요? | Is there gochujang in the sauce? |
| 간장이랑 고추장에도 밀이 들어가는 경우가 많아요. | Soy sauce and gochujang often contain wheat too. |
| 밀가루뿐 아니라 밀도 안 돼요. | Not just wheat flour — wheat itself is a problem too. |

That last one is a statement, not a question, and it is often the sentence that changes the conversation.

**The rule we actually run on: unless a commercial bottle or tub says otherwise, assume it contains wheat.** That is what years of buying these in Korean supermarkets has taught us — in our experience the default soy sauce and gochujang on the shelf are wheat-bearing, and the ones without wheat say so on the label, because for them it's a selling point. We're stating a shopping habit, not a survey of the category: what it means in practice is that a silent label sends you to the ingredient list, not past it.

The exception is a labelled category, not a lucky bottle. **양조간장** (brewed soy sauce) is fermented with wheat and is the standard restaurant pour — Sempio's own page for its [501 brewed soy sauce](https://en.sempio.com/product/soysauce/view/598) lists wheat among the ingredients and declares "wheat, soybeans" as allergens (retrieved August 2026). Traditional **조선간장 / 한식간장** — the meju-based kind used for soups and namul — carries no wheat *as the category is traditionally made*, from soybean meju and salt. We have not checked individual bottles on Korean shelves against that definition, and **혼합간장** (blended) products sit right beside them, so treat the category as a reason to read the ingredient list rather than as a clearance. And none of this is identifiable from a finished dish, which is why the question still has to be asked out loud. Why these condiments carry wheat at all is covered in [hidden gluten in Korean food](/blog/hidden-gluten-korean-food).

### 4. Korean BBQ

Grilled meat is the most workable restaurant category in Korea: a plain cut of pork or beef carries no wheat of its own, so the risk moves to the marinade, the table sauces, and the shared grill. What you can't see from your seat is whether a "plain" cut was brined or tenderised before it reached the table — so ask rather than assume.

| Korean | English |
|---|---|
| 양념 안 한 고기 있나요? | Do you have unseasoned meat? |
| 쌈장은 빼 주세요. | No ssamjang, please. |
| 불판 새것으로 바꿔 주실 수 있나요? | Could you change to a fresh grill plate? |
| 집게랑 가위도 새것으로 부탁드려요. | New tongs and scissors as well, please. |
| 혹시 미리 염지하거나 양념하셨나요? | Was it brined or seasoned beforehand? |

Ssamjang is built on doenjang and gochujang, so it goes. Treat any grill that has been cooking soy-marinated meat as contaminated.

### 5. Bakeries and cafés

| Korean | English |
|---|---|
| 이 빵 100% 쌀가루인가요? | Is this bread 100% rice flour? |
| 밀가루 조금이라도 섞나요? | Is any wheat flour mixed in at all? |

Ask both. **쌀빵** (rice bread) describes what went into the dough, not what stayed out of it — during our first year back in Korea (2025–26), one bakery owner told us — cheerfully, without being pressed — that his was 10% wheat. He wasn't hiding it; nobody had ever asked. The percentage question is the one that works.

### 6. If you have coeliac disease

Everything above is written from our household's reality, which is non-coeliac gluten sensitivity. If you're coeliac, you need a stricter conversation than we run, and these are the sentences for it:

| Korean | English |
|---|---|
| 저는 셀리악병이라서 아주 조금만 들어가도 아파요. | I have coeliac disease — even a tiny amount makes me ill. |
| 같은 팬에 조리하면 안 돼요. | It can't be cooked in the same pan. |
| 튀김기 따로 쓰시나요? | Do you use a separate fryer? |
| 밀, 보리, 호밀 전부 안 돼요. | No wheat, barley, or rye — none of them. |

That last line matters more in Korea than it might elsewhere. Korea's mandatory allergen declaration, set by the MFDS labelling standard [식품등의 표시기준](https://www.law.go.kr/행정규칙/식품등의표시기준) (고시 제2025-60호, 29 August 2025 — [notice page](https://www.mfds.go.kr/brd/m_211/view.do?seq=14917), retrieved August 2026), **covers wheat. Barley (보리) and rye (호밀) are not on it.** So "no 밀" is a narrower answer here than a coeliac reader will assume. Naming all three closes that gap in conversation; on packaging you have to close it yourself by reading the full ingredient list, which is the method in [our Korean label guide](/blog/reading-korean-food-labels).

## The block to save before you fly

Copy this and keep it in your notes app, or screenshot it. It's the version to hand over when you'd rather give the whole picture at once than go back and forth:

> 안녕하세요. 제가 밀 알러지가 있어요.  
> 밀가루뿐 아니라 밀이 들어간 건 전부 못 먹어요. 보리, 호밀도 안 돼요.  
> 간장이나 고추장에도 밀이 들어가는 경우가 많아서요.  
> 이 음식에 들어가는지 주방에 확인해 주실 수 있을까요?  
> 감사합니다!

*"Hello. I have a wheat allergy. Not just wheat flour — anything with wheat in it, and barley and rye too. Soy sauce and gochujang often contain wheat. Could you check with the kitchen whether this dish has any? Thank you!"*

Note the difference from the opener: the short phrase says **밀가루** (wheat flour) because that is the word that gets a kitchen moving, while this longer one says **밀** (wheat) because it has room to close the gap. Use the opener to start the conversation and this block when you want the whole picture in one pass.

Two habits that came out of doing this badly first: **lead with the allergy, not the request** — starting with what you can't eat sets the frame before the menu negotiation begins. And **don't paste 글루텐프리 into a translation app and show that.** It's grammatical; it's just answering a question nobody in the kitchen is equipped to answer.

## What these phrases can't do

They change what the kitchen *tells* you. They don't change what the kitchen *does*.

- A phrase can't inspect a shared fryer, a grill plate, or a prep surface. For coeliac disease, cross-contamination is the larger risk and it survives a perfectly honest "no wheat."
- Staff turn over. An answer confirmed on one visit is not a standing property of the restaurant — we re-ask every time.
- Manufacturers reformulate without notice, so a sauce that was fine last year may not be now.
- We are describing what has worked for us in Seoul and Gyeonggi as a couple managing NCGS. It is first-hand, and it is not a survey — no sample, no measurement, and no lab test behind any of it.

**How to check independently:** for packaged food, read the 원재료명 (ingredient list) yourself using the [four-word scan](/blog/reading-korean-food-labels) — that's verifiable in your hand and doesn't require trusting us or the staff. For a restaurant, phone ahead: the same phrases work on a call, and a kitchen that has time to think gives a better answer than one mid-rush.
