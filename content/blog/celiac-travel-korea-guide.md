## About My Wife — and Why This Isn't a Strict Celiac Guide

Before anything else: my wife has **non-celiac gluten sensitivity (NCGS)**. She is not celiac. She reacts badly to gluten — vomiting, bloating, diarrhea — but she tolerates trace amounts that would make a celiac person seriously ill.

That distinction matters for how you read this post.

Everything we recommend works for NCGS. Most of it also works for celiac travelers, but **not always**. When I say "this place is fine," I mean fine for her. If you're celiac, you should apply stricter standards than we do: ask about shared fryers, cross-contamination, dedicated prep surfaces. Call ahead. Assume every "gluten-free" label in Korea is actually "low-gluten" until proven otherwise.

The short version of how we got here: she developed gluten sensitivity in the UK, we spent years in the US where I taught myself gluten-free Korean cooking from scratch — soy sauce, gochujang, everything from the pantry up.

When we moved back to Korea last year, I thought we'd have it easier. We didn't. What we did have, for the first time, was leverage: my wife's aunt makes soy sauce and gochujang at home, without wheat, the traditional way. That buffer gave us time to figure out which restaurants to trust and which "gluten-free" bakeries to walk past.

This site — [noglutenkorea.com](https://noglutenkorea.com) — is the directory we wished existed when we landed. This post is the map.

## What Korean Food Actually Looks Like, From a Gluten Angle

The short version: almost everything has soy sauce.

**Soy sauce** (간장, *ganjang*) in Korea is brewed from wheat and soybeans. It's the base of most marinades, banchan (side dishes), and cooking liquids. Bulgogi, galbi, japchae, bibimbap sauce, most stew bases — all soy sauce. Skipping soy sauce in Korea is like skipping olive oil in Italy.

**Gochujang** (고추장, red pepper paste) is usually made with wheat flour or barley. **Doenjang** (된장, soybean paste) often includes wheat. **Ssamjang** (쌈장), the classic Korean BBQ dipping sauce, is gochujang and doenjang combined. If it's red or brown, assume wheat until you've checked.

Then there's a second tier of hidden gluten:

- **Eomuk** (어묵, fish cake) is usually wheat starch. A few brands, notably Goraesa (고래사), make wheat-free versions, but the default on a stew or a street stall is wheat.
- **Tteokbokki** (떡볶이, spicy rice cake dish): the rice cakes themselves are GF, but the sauce contains gochujang and the fish cakes mixed in have wheat.
- **Fried chicken** in Korea is almost always wheat-battered. There are rice-based brands (we'll get to ssaltongdak below) but they're rare.
- **Ramyeon** is wheat noodles, full stop. Every convenience-store cup, every late-night ramyeon bar. Rice-based Korean ramen exists but is niche.

And then there's the marketing trap.

Korea's gluten-free wave is mostly diet-trend marketing, not celiac awareness. I've asked bakery owners under "Gluten-Free" neon signs about their flour ratio and been told cheerfully: "Oh, 90% rice flour, 10% wheat flour." I once ordered a loaf of "gluten-free bread" online; a week later the product name had been quietly changed to "rice bread" — no asterisk, no apology. Don't trust the label. Trust the ingredient list, or a dedicated GF operator you can talk to.

## Where We Actually Eat

We think about Seoul's gluten-free scene in three tiers.

**Tier 1: Dedicated GF.** Everything on the menu is gluten-free. These are the places we actually relax in — where we don't need to interrogate staff, just order. There are only a handful in Korea.

**Tier 2: GF-Friendly.** Not gluten-free overall, but the kitchen understands the request and has safe menu items we've tested. We still ask every visit; staff turnover is real.

**Tier 3: Regular restaurants we've made work.** Most Korean restaurants, ultimately. Grilled meat, plain rice, unseasoned vegetables — possible with the right phrases. We'll cover those in the communication section below.

Here's what's at the top.

### Tier 1 — Dedicated Gluten-Free

**237 Pizza** (237피자) — Hannam-dong, Yongsan-gu, B1

The only GFCO-certified gluten-free pizza in Korea, as far as we've been able to confirm. The owner has celiac disease himself, and opened 237 Pizza after years of research — partly out of frustration that no one else in Korea had.

237 was on our must-visit list the moment we landed in Seoul. I was skeptical at first — "GF pizza" is a phrase that has burned me enough times — but after one slice of their artichoke pizza I gave up skepticism. The crust isn't apologetic. It's a pizza that happens to be gluten-free, not the other way around.

![237 Pizza in Hannam — the only GFCO-certified gluten-free pizza in Korea](/images/places/237-pizza/01.webp)
*237 Pizza, Hannam. Direct photo from visit.*

We order the **Quattro Funghi** (mushroom) every visit. If you're there on a weekend evening, expect a wait. The interior is warm, old-pop music plays, and it feels like a place the owner actually wants to sit in. For the full writeup see our [gluten-free restaurants roundup](/blog/gluten-free-restaurants-seoul).

**monil2 house** (모닐2하우스) — Yeonnam-dong, Mapo-gu

A dedicated gluten-free bakery and cafe in Yeonnam, one of the more walkable neighborhoods near Hongik. Bread, cakes, desserts — the full menu is GF. The bread is better than it has any right to be; most GF bakeries in Seoul produce something closer to rice-flour cake, but monil2 has figured out a loaf with real structure. Cozy interior, small, usually worth the short walk from Hongik Station.

![monil2 house in Yeonnam-dong — dedicated gluten-free bakery and cafe](/images/places/monil2-house/01.webp)
*monil2 house, Yeonnam. Direct photo.*

What sets monil2 apart is how much the owner actually cares. He keeps a stack of printed GF guidance cards in the shop — Korean phrases to show restaurants, translations, the kind of thing that saves a traveler a meal. He also sells travel-sized packets of gluten-free gochujang and gluten-free soy sauce, which you can throw in a bag before a Korean BBQ night. Our picks: the **churros** (with his own chocolate dip) and the **bulgogi pizza**.

Full writeup in our [GF bakeries & cafes post](/blog/gluten-free-bakeries-cafes-seoul).

**Cafe Rebirth** — Godeok, Pyeongtaek (Gyeonggi)

A fully GF cafe about an hour south of Seoul, near the US military base at Camp Humphreys. The entire menu is gluten-free, and the seasonal fruit cakes are the signature. It has a large parking lot, which — if you drive in Korea — will mean more to you than any other piece of information on this page. Good stop if you're heading out of the city or visiting someone at the base.

### Tier 2 — GF-Friendly Standouts

These are places where the kitchen understands the request and has items we've personally confirmed. Still ask every visit.

**Dark & Light** — Geumto-dong, Seongnam (Gyeonggi)

Western fine dining with unusually thoughtful allergen accommodation. The staff walks through every ingredient if you ask, and they'll modify dishes if you explain. Their **potato lasagna** and **Hanwoo steak** have both been safe for us across multiple visits. Reservations required. Large parking lot, nice for a date night.

**Grain Seoul** — Sinchon, Seodaemun-gu, Seoul

A brunch spot with oatmeal pancakes and sandwiches that can be modified with GF bread swaps. Not every item is marked GF on the menu, but the staff will adjust when asked. Second-floor walk-up (no elevator); write your name on the pad on the table and wait. Walk-ins are fine, but busy weekends will have you waiting.

**Cafe Pepper** — Songridan-gil, Songpa-gu, Seoul

A beautiful dessert cafe on Songpa's Songridan-gil. Our standing order is the **matcha pound cake** (confirmed GF). A few other items are labeled "low-gluten" — that is NOT the same as GF, and we skip those. Always confirm directly with staff. Reservations via the CatchTable app, or just walk in. Second floor, no elevator, busy on weekends.

**benir** — Magok, Gangseo-gu, Seoul

A GF cafe with gorgeous cakes — strawberry, chocolate, seasonal fruit — on the second floor of the Magok Sciencestar Building. Most of the menu is gluten-free. Watch out for items that use commercial snack toppings (their Ppotto crumble cake, for example), which may contain gluten. Interior is warm, full of plants, Marshall speaker in the corner.

**ssaltongdak** (쌀통닭) — Dangsan, Yeongdeungpo-gu, Seoul

Rice-flour fried chicken. The **plain fried chicken** and the puffed rice crackers (*ppeongttwigi*) that come with it are GF-safe. I called the franchise headquarters directly to confirm: only the plain fried is gluten-free; anything sauced (*yangnyeom*, *ganjang*, teriyaki-style) contains gluten. Individual branches may have cross-contamination depending on kitchen setup, so confirm at your location. A rice-flour pizza is also on their menu — we haven't personally verified it.

## The One Phrase That Actually Works

If you remember one sentence from this guide, make it this:

> **제가 밀가루 알러지가 있어서요. 밀가루 들어가나요?**
> *Jega milgaru alleogiga isseoseoyo. Milgaru deureoganayo?*
> "I have a wheat allergy. Does this contain wheat flour?"

The framing is the whole point. Most Korean restaurant staff don't know "gluten-free" (글루텐프리) — the word exists, but not the concept. But they understand **밀가루** (wheat flour), and they understand **알러지** (allergy). Frame your request as an allergy and the kitchen actually checks. They walk back, ask the chef, come back with a real answer.

This isn't overstating. In a year of eating out as a couple with one GF person, the single biggest shift in quality of service has been swapping "글루텐프리" for "밀가루 알러지." Every time.

Two practical notes:

**Screenshot the phrase.** Showing Korean text on your phone beats pronunciation. Half the time we just hold up the phone.

**Reconfirm once.** If the first answer is a breezy "안 들어가요~" ("no, doesn't contain it"), follow up: *"제가 알러지가 있어서요, 한 번만 더 확인해주실 수 있나요?"* ("I have an allergy — could you double-check?"). That's when the server actually walks to the kitchen. When in doubt, remind them of the hidden sources — soy sauce (간장), gochujang (고추장), doenjang (된장). A lot of Korean cooks think of these as "seasoning," not "wheat."

## Reading Korean Labels: Four Words That Matter

At the convenience store and the mart, the product label is your most honest friend.

Ingredient lists in Korea are printed on the back or bottom of the packaging. The format is consistent: raw ingredients in descending order, then a dedicated line for **알레르기 유발물질** ("allergens") which lists the major 22 flagged allergens for that product.

These are the words to scan for:

- **밀** — wheat
- **소맥** / **소맥분** — also wheat (same thing, different wording)
- **보리** — barley
- **호밀** — rye

If any appear, put the product back. There's also a common cross-contamination disclaimer:

> "이 제품은 밀을 사용한 제품과 같은 시설에서 제조"
> "Manufactured in the same facility as products containing wheat."

For NCGS we make case-by-case calls on these. For celiac travelers, avoid entirely — shared equipment is real risk.

One trap: "**쌀로 만든**" ("made with rice") does NOT mean gluten-free. Rice-labeled products often contain wheat starch as a binder. Check the ingredient list, not the packaging headline.

My wife's scan-and-flip routine is roughly two seconds per item now: pick up, flip, search for 밀 in the allergen line, return or keep. That's the entire operation. Walkthrough with screenshots in our [Korean food label guide](/blog/reading-korean-food-labels).

## Convenience Store Survival

Korea's convenience stores (CU, GS25, 7-Eleven, Emart24) are incredible. They're also, for gluten-free people, a minefield of triangle kimbap, cup ramen, and fried everything.

Here's what reliably works across chains:

- **Boiled eggs** (삶은 계란) — naturally GF, protein-dense, everywhere.
- **Bananas and fresh fruit** — the food of the GF traveler.
- **Plain yogurt** — check flavored versions; some have wheat-based thickeners.
- **Packaged grilled chicken breast** (닭가슴살) — skip anything marinated in soy sauce; plain is usually safe.
- **Some rice snacks** — Chamsal Seolbyeong (참쌀설병) in the **pink** package is safe. The green package contains wheat.
- **A handful of chips that usually pass** — Poca Chip Original (NOT onion flavor), Doritos cheese (NOT other flavors), Banana Kick, Yegam (all flavors), plain corn chips. Always re-verify on the actual packet.

What we skip entirely: triangle kimbap (too many variants with soy sauce), dosirak lunch boxes (wheat somewhere in most), instant ramen cups, frozen dumplings, most sausages.

## FAQ

**Is Korean food gluten-free by default?**
No. It looks rice-based, but soy sauce, gochujang, doenjang, and wheat-based noodles are in most dishes. Even "just meat and rice" plates usually have soy sauce in the marinade.

**Can I eat Korean BBQ gluten-free?**
Yes, with prep. Ask for unseasoned meat ("양념 안 한 고기 있나요?"), bring GF soy sauce, and skip sauced banchan sides. Plain salt-grilled samgyeopsal is reliably safe.

**Is any restaurant in Seoul truly dedicated gluten-free?**
Yes, but few. 237 Pizza (Hannam), monil2 house (Yeonnam), and Cafe Rebirth (Pyeongtaek) are the three we trust most. See our [restaurants list](/blog/gluten-free-restaurants-seoul) and [bakeries list](/blog/gluten-free-bakeries-cafes-seoul) for the full vetted set.

**Which Korean soy sauce is gluten-free?**
Commercial Korean soy sauces almost all contain wheat. Imported Japanese tamari is the easiest GF substitute in Seoul (available at Lotte Mart, larger Emart, Kim's Club). For long-term residents, traditional home-brewed soy sauce (집간장) without wheat is the classic option.

**Can I buy gluten-free bread in Korea?**
Yes, but verify. Many "rice bread" shops blend 10-30% wheat flour into the dough. Only commit to dedicated GF bakeries like monil2 house, Cafe Rebirth, and similar. Full list in our [GF bakeries & cafes guide](/blog/gluten-free-bakeries-cafes-seoul).

## A Final Word

Korea is worth it. The food culture is one of the richest in the world, and with a little preparation you can eat safely and enjoyably — not just survive.

The biggest shift for us, after a year, has been mental. Eating out in Korea as a GF couple isn't a puzzle we have to solve every night; it's a set of routines. Allergy framing. Phone screenshots. Flip-and-scan. GF tamari in the bag. A short list of places we can walk into without thinking.

If you're traveling here, or moving here, the learning curve is real but shorter than it looks. Start with the Tier 1 anchors. Memorize the allergy phrase. Keep a mental map of the convenience-store basics. You'll develop your own instincts within a couple of weeks.
