If you take one thing from this post, let it be this: in Korean food, the danger is almost never the dish you're looking at. It's the **condiments inside it.**

My wife has non-celiac gluten sensitivity, and for our first stretch in Korea we tried to solve this the way most guides tell you to — by finding safe restaurants. Then 237 Pizza, our anchor spot, went dark for a remodel, and we found ourselves cooking at home more and more. That turned out to be the real education. Once you're the one holding the soy sauce bottle and reading the back label, you finally see where gluten actually lives in this cuisine. Here's the map we wish we'd had on day one.

## The fermented condiment problem (this is the big one)

Korean cooking is built on a handful of fermented condiments, and most of them contain wheat:

| Condiment | Gluten? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 간장 (soy sauce, brewed) | **Yes** | Fermented from wheat + soybeans |
| 된장 (soybean paste) | **Usually** | Wheat often in the ingredient list |
| 고추장 (chili paste) | **Usually** | Made with wheat flour or barley |
| 쌈장 (dipping paste) | **Yes** | Built on doenjang + gochujang |
| 양조식초 (brewed vinegar) | Verify | Can be wheat-based |

Read that table again and think about how many Korean dishes touch at least one of these. Marinated meat, stews, stir-fries, dipping sauces, banchan, "just a little sauce" on top — the base layer of Korean flavor is, by default, a gluten source. This is why a plate that looks perfectly safe can still get you: the wheat isn't a visible ingredient, it's dissolved into the seasoning.

It's also why, at restaurants, we learned to say **"밀가루 알러지"** (wheat allergy) rather than "gluten-free." Staff who cheerfully wave off "gluten" will actually go check the kitchen when you say allergy — and they'll remember to think about the soy sauce and the gochujang, which almost nobody counts as "having flour in it."

## Second-tier hidden sources

Beyond the condiments, a cluster of everyday foods carry wheat you wouldn't guess:

- **어묵 / 오뎅 (fish cake)** — bound with wheat starch, nearly always. A handful of brands (e.g. Goraesa) make wheat-free versions, but assume standard fish cake is out until the label proves otherwise.
- **떡볶이 (tteokbokki)** — the rice cakes are fine; the *sauce* (gochujang) and the *fish cake* are the problem. A GF-condiment version at home is easy; the street-stall version is not.
- **카레 루 (curry roux)** — Japanese-style Korean curry blocks are thickened with wheat flour.
- **소시지 · 핫도그 (sausages, hot dogs)** — wheat is a common filler/binder.
- **드레싱 · 소스 (dressings, sauces)** — wheat-derived thickeners are common; sesame and Japanese-style dressings are frequent offenders.
- **한국식 치킨 (Korean fried chicken)** — the batter is wheat by default. The exception is rice-flour fried-chicken shops — but even there, *only the plain fried version;* the sauced (양념) chicken usually reintroduces wheat through the sauce.

## Noodles: mostly wheat, with two bright spots

Korean noodle culture is a wheat minefield: **라면 (ramen)** is wheat, **칼국수** is wheat, **잔치국수** is wheat. Rice-based instant noodles exist but are genuinely rare.

The two things worth memorizing:

- **당면 (dangmyeon), the glass noodle in japchae, is made from sweet potato starch — naturally gluten-free.** The catch is the sauce (soy sauce), not the noodle.
- **떡 (rice cake) itself is gluten-free.** Again: it's what's *around* it that gets you.

That pattern — a naturally safe base wrapped in an unsafe sauce — is the whole story of Korean gluten. It's also exactly why cooking at home changes everything: swap in gluten-free versions of a few condiments and huge swaths of the cuisine open back up.

## The "10% wheat flour" trick

Here's the Korea-specific trap that catches even careful people. Korea's "gluten-free" boom is mostly diet-and-wellness marketing, not celiac awareness — and the labeling reflects that.

- "쌀빵 (rice bread)" shops have exploded, but many blend **10–30% wheat flour** into a dough they still market as rice-based.
- A **"저글루텐 / low-gluten"** label is *not* gluten-free. For celiac and NCGS alike, it's not safe.
- I once ordered a loaf advertised as "gluten-free" online; a week later the product name had been quietly changed to "rice bread" — no asterisk, no notice. The wheat had presumably been there all along.

The rule we live by: a "gluten-free" claim in Korea means "probably low-gluten" until a person you can talk to, or a real certification (GFCO / Korea GF), proves otherwise.

## Celiac vs. NCGS: how careful is careful enough?

Everything above is written from our reality, which is non-celiac gluten sensitivity. For NCGS, the goal is avoiding wheat as an ingredient — a wheat-free soy sauce and a kitchen that doesn't cook with flour go a long way.

If you're **celiac**, apply a stricter standard than we do. The condiment problem is worse for you, because it's not just *"is there wheat in this soy sauce"* but *"was this pan wiped with the same cloth as the wheat-battered chicken."* Trace amounts that don't bother my wife can genuinely harm a celiac. So: assume shared fryers and shared surfaces unless told otherwise, treat "저글루텐" as unsafe, and lean hard on dedicated-GF sources and home cooking where you control the whole chain. When in doubt, this whole cuisine rewards the person who cooks it themselves.

## The good news: control the condiments, reclaim the cuisine

It's easy to read all this as "Korean food is off-limits." It isn't. Look again and you'll notice how much is *naturally* gluten-free the moment you remove the wheat-bearing seasoning: plain grilled meat, steamed rice, many vegetable banchan, dangmyeon, rice cake, eggs, tofu, most soups built on plain broth rather than soy sauce.

That's the whole case for cooking at home — and why our own gluten-free life in Korea shifted from chasing safe restaurants to stocking a safe kitchen. When you own the soy sauce, the gochujang, and the batter, the "hidden" gluten stops being hidden. (The single biggest unlock for us was a supply of genuinely wheat-free soy sauce and gochujang — more on sourcing those in a coming post.)

## What to do with this

1. **Treat the fermented condiments as guilty until proven innocent** — soy sauce, gochujang, doenjang, ssamjang.
2. **Learn to flip and scan the label** for 밀 / 소맥 / 소맥분 (wheat), 보리 (barley), 호밀 (rye). Full method in our [label-reading guide](/blog/reading-korean-food-labels).
3. **Cook the naturally-safe base, control the sauce.** Rice, dangmyeon, plain-grilled protein, and vegetables get you surprisingly far.
4. When you *do* eat out, use the [restaurant list](/blog/gluten-free-restaurants-seoul) and say "wheat allergy," not "gluten-free."
