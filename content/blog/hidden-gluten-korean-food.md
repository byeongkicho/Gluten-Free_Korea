*Updated August 2026: anchored the soy sauce claim to the manufacturer's own ingredient listing, added the mandatory-allergen gap for barley and rye, removed a percentage figure we could not source, named the wheat-free condiments actually in our kitchen — and corrected a real error, since an earlier version of this piece cleared 떡 (rice cake) as a category when 밀떡 is made with wheat flour.*

If you take one thing from this post, let it be this: in Korean food, the danger is almost never the dish you're looking at. It's the **condiments inside it.**

My wife has non-celiac gluten sensitivity, and for our first stretch in Korea we tried to solve this the way most guides tell you to — by finding safe restaurants. Then 237 Pizza in Hannam, our anchor spot, closed for a stretch to remodel, and we found ourselves cooking at home more and more. That turned out to be the real education. Once you're the one holding the soy sauce bottle and reading the back label, you finally see where gluten actually lives in this cuisine. Here's the map we wish we'd had on day one.

**Where this comes from, and what it can't tell you.** The condiment map below is what we look for on the 원재료명 (*wonjaeryomyeong*, ingredient list) of the products we cook with, plus the manufacturer and regulatory sources linked at each claim. It is not a brand survey — we have not checked every product in a category, formulations differ between product lines carrying the same brand name, and Korean manufacturers reformulate without redesigning the package. And reading an ingredient list establishes only what a maker declared: it cannot establish that a product is free of trace gluten from shared equipment. Use this to know *what to look for*; the label in your hand is the answer.

## The fermented condiment problem (this is the big one)

Korean cooking is built on a handful of fermented condiments, and most of them contain wheat:

| Condiment | Wheat? | What to look for on the label |
|---|---|---|
| 간장 (*ganjang*, brewed soy sauce) | **Yes, by design** | Sempio's own page for [양조간장 501](https://en.sempio.com/product/soysauce/view/598) lists wheat among the ingredients and declares "wheat, soybeans" as allergens (retrieved August 2026) |
| 고추장 (*gochujang*, chili paste) | **Often** | wheat can enter by more than one route — 소맥분 (wheat flour), 밀쌀 (*milssal*, hulled wheat), or 양조간장 itself listed as an ingredient. Check for all three |
| 된장 (*doenjang*, soybean paste) | **Sometimes** | traditionally soybeans, salt and water; commercial tubs sometimes list 밀. Not a category you can rule in or out — a per-tub check |
| 쌈장 (*ssamjang*, dipping paste) | **Inherits** | built on doenjang plus gochujang, so it carries whatever those carry |
| 양조식초 (*yangjo-sikcho*, brewed vinegar) | **Check** | can be brewed from wheat |

**A trap worth naming:** the answer is set by the *product*, not the brand. One brand name can cover a line built on 소맥분 and a line built on rice, under product names a shelf-shopper reads as interchangeable. Memorising a brand is how people get caught; reading the tub is how they don't.

Read that table again and think about how many Korean dishes touch at least one of these. Marinated meat, stews, stir-fries, dipping sauces, banchan, "just a little sauce" on top — the base layer of Korean flavor is, by default, a gluten source. This is why a plate that looks perfectly safe can still get you: the wheat isn't a visible ingredient, it's dissolved into the seasoning.

It's also why, at restaurants, we learned to say **"밀가루 알러지"** (wheat allergy) rather than "gluten-free." Staff who cheerfully wave off "gluten" will actually go check the kitchen when you say allergy — and they'll remember to think about the soy sauce and the gochujang, which almost nobody counts as "having flour in it."

## The allergen line only covers wheat

Before any of the checks below, know the gap in what the package guarantees. Korean packaged food carries an allergen line (**알레르기 유발물질**), and the substances it must declare are set by the MFDS labeling standard, [식품등의 표시기준](https://www.law.go.kr/행정규칙/식품등의표시기준) (별표 2 — current text as retrieved August 2026; the standard is amended regularly). **밀 (wheat) is on that list. 보리 (barley) and 호밀 (rye) are not.**

So a clean allergen line rules out declared wheat and nothing else. Barley shows up only if it's spelled out in the full 원재료명 — and barley is not a corner case here: it appears in gochujang, in malt syrups, in 보리차. Checking the bolded allergen box and stopping there is the most common way a careful reader still gets caught. The full four-step method is in our [label-reading guide](/blog/reading-korean-food-labels).

## Second-tier hidden sources

Beyond the condiments, a cluster of everyday foods carry wheat you wouldn't guess:

- **어묵 / 오뎅 (*eomuk*, fish cake)** — bound with wheat starch, nearly always. Goraesa (고래사) is the name that comes up most often in Korean gluten-free discussion as making wheat-free versions, but we have not read a specific Goraesa label ourselves — treat that as a lead to check, not a clearance. The default on a stew or a street stall is wheat.
- **떡볶이 (*tteokbokki*)** — three separate checks, not one: the tteok itself (밀떡 is wheat — see the noodles section below), the *sauce* (gochujang), and the *fish cake*. A version at home with a checked bag of 쌀떡 and checked condiments is easy; the street-stall version is not.
- **카레 루 (*kare-ru*, curry roux)** — Japanese-style Korean curry blocks are thickened with wheat flour.
- **소시지 · 핫도그 (sausages, hot dogs)** — wheat is a common filler/binder.
- **드레싱 · 소스 (dressings, sauces)** — wheat-derived thickeners are common; sesame and Japanese-style dressings are frequent offenders.
- **한국식 치킨 (Korean fried chicken)** — the batter is wheat by default. Rice-flour fried-chicken shops exist — but even there, the sauced (양념, *yangnyeom*) version usually reintroduces wheat through the sauce, so the plain fried version is the only one worth asking about.

## Noodles: mostly wheat, with two bright spots

Korean noodle culture is a wheat minefield: **라면** (*ramyeon*) is wheat, **칼국수** (*kalguksu*) is wheat, **잔치국수** (*janchi-guksu*) is wheat. Rice-based instant noodles exist but are genuinely rare.

The two things worth memorizing:

- **당면 (*dangmyeon*), the glass noodle in japchae, is sweet potato starch — naturally gluten-free.** Two catches: the sauce (soy sauce) rather than the noodle, and the fact that some commercial dangmyeon is a mixed-starch product, so the 원재료명 is still worth a look.
- **떡 (*tteok*) made from rice is gluten-free — but "rice cake" is not a category you can clear.** Tteokbokki tteok comes in two kinds: 쌀떡 (*ssal-tteok*, rice) and **밀떡 (*mil-tteok*), made with wheat flour** — a mainstream product, sold in supermarkets and used at street stalls. 쌀떡파 vs 밀떡파 — rice-cake faction versus wheat-cake faction — is a running argument in Korea, which tells you how normal 밀떡 is. So the bag has to be read like any other package: check the 원재료명 for 밀 or 소맥분 before you cook with it.

That pattern — a naturally safe base wrapped in an unsafe sauce — is the whole story of Korean gluten. It's also exactly why cooking at home changes everything: swap in gluten-free versions of a few condiments and huge swaths of the cuisine open back up.

## The "rice bread" trap

Here's the Korea-specific trap that catches even careful people. **쌀빵** (*ssalppang*, rice bread) shops have multiplied, and "made with rice" describes what went *into* the dough — it is not a claim about what stayed out. Rice breads we've picked up have listed wheat flour in the 원재료명 right alongside the rice.

- A **"저글루텐 / low-gluten"** label is *not* gluten-free. For celiac and NCGS alike, low-gluten is still gluten.
- **We went looking for a Korean equivalent of [GFCO](https://gfco.org)** — a third-party body that audits a facility and licenses a seal — and as of August 2026 we have not found one. That's an absence we searched for rather than a proof that none exists; if you know of one, tell us and we'll correct this. What it means in practice: a front-of-package gluten-free claim here tells you what the maker states, not what an outside body checked.
- I once ordered a loaf advertised as "gluten-free" online; a week later the product name had been quietly changed to "rice bread" — no asterisk, no notice.

That last one is the reason for the standing rule in our kitchen: a front-label claim is where the checking starts, and the 원재료명 is where it finishes.

## Celiac vs. NCGS: how careful is careful enough?

Everything above is written from our reality, which is non-celiac gluten sensitivity. For NCGS, the goal is avoiding wheat as an ingredient — a wheat-free soy sauce and a kitchen that doesn't cook with flour go a long way.

If you're **celiac**, apply a stricter standard than we do. The condiment problem is worse for you, because it's not just *"is there wheat in this soy sauce"* but *"was this pan wiped with the same cloth as the wheat-battered chicken."* Trace amounts that don't bother my wife can genuinely harm a celiac. So: assume shared fryers, shared woks and shared surfaces unless told otherwise, treat "저글루텐" as unsafe, and lean hard on dedicated-GF sources and home cooking where you control the whole chain. A shared wok is the one that catches japchae and stir-fries — the dish can be built entirely from safe ingredients and still come out of a pan that fried something battered. When in doubt, this whole cuisine rewards the person who cooks it themselves.

## The good news: control the condiments, reclaim the cuisine

It's easy to read all this as "Korean food is off-limits." It isn't. Look again and you'll notice how much is *naturally* gluten-free the moment you remove the wheat-bearing seasoning: plain grilled meat, steamed rice, many vegetable banchan, dangmyeon and rice-flour tteok (both label-checked), eggs, tofu, most soups built on plain broth rather than soy sauce.

That's the whole case for cooking at home — and why our own gluten-free life in Korea shifted from chasing safe restaurants to stocking a safe kitchen. When you own the soy sauce, the gochujang, and the batter, the "hidden" gluten stops being hidden.

The single biggest unlock was a standing supply of wheat-free versions of the two condiments at the top of that table. What that actually looks like for us:

- **Soy sauce → imported Japanese tamari.** The easiest wheat-free substitute to find in Seoul; Lotte Mart, the larger Emart branches and Kim's Club carry it. Tamari is a category, not a guarantee — some tamari includes a small amount of wheat — so read that bottle too. What you want on the 원재료명 is soybeans, salt, water and often alcohol, with no 소맥 or 밀 anywhere in the list.
- **The traditional route → 집간장** (*jip-ganjang*), home-brewed soy sauce, which is soybeans and salt without the wheat that goes into commercial 양조간장. My wife's aunt brews both soy sauce and gochujang this way, which is the buffer that bought us time to figure the rest out. Not something you can buy — but it's the term to ask about.
- **For a trip → monil2 house** in Yeonnam sells travel-sized packets of gluten-free gochujang and soy sauce — the version that fits in a bag before a Korean BBQ night. That's from our own visit, not from a label we've read or a certificate we've checked, so the rule above applies here as well: the packet's claim is where the checking starts. Ask at the shop, and read the 원재료명.

## What to do with this

1. **Treat the fermented condiments as guilty until proven innocent** — soy sauce, gochujang, doenjang, ssamjang.
2. **Learn to flip and scan the label** for 밀 / 소맥 / 소맥분 (wheat), 보리 (barley), 호밀 (rye) — and don't stop at the bolded allergen line, which only has to declare wheat. Full method in our [label-reading guide](/blog/reading-korean-food-labels).
3. **Cook the naturally-safe base, control the sauce.** Rice, dangmyeon, plain-grilled protein, and vegetables get you surprisingly far.
4. When you *do* eat out, use the [restaurant list](/blog/gluten-free-restaurants-seoul) and say "wheat allergy," not "gluten-free."
