Ask me to do one thing in a Korean convenience store and I'll do it without thinking: pick up a new snack, flip it over, and scan the back for one word — **밀** (wheat). It takes about two seconds now. But it took months of getting it wrong to make it automatic, and it's the single most useful habit we built while learning to eat gluten-free in Korea.

Restaurants are a conversation. Packaged food is a *reading test* — and once you can read the label, a huge amount of Korean grocery and convenience-store food opens back up. Here's the exact routine.

## Why Korean labels are their own skill

Two things make Korean packaging different from what you might be used to:

1. **The ingredient list is in Korean, and so are the allergen words.** You can't rely on spotting "wheat" in English — you have to recognize the Hangul.
2. **"Gluten-free" on the front is voluntary and loosely used.** Korea's gluten-free labeling isn't tied to celiac-grade certification the way GFCO is. Plenty of products marketed as "저글루텐 (low-gluten)" or loosely "gluten-free" still contain wheat. (More on that trap in [the hidden-gluten guide](/blog/hidden-gluten-korean-food).)

Korean law helps — but only halfway. Processed foods must declare major allergens, and **wheat is one of them**, so wheat almost always turns up in a predictable spot. The catch that trips up even careful people: **barley and rye are *not* mandatory allergens in Korea.** Those you have to hunt for yourself. That distinction is the whole reason this routine has four steps instead of one.

## The 4-step flip-and-scan for gluten

### Step 1 — Flip it and find the ingredient list

Turn the package over. Look for **원재료명** (ingredient list) — sometimes printed as **원재료명 및 함량** (ingredients and amounts), usually on the back or the bottom. By law, processed foods have to print it. (Don't confuse it with 영양성분, the nutrition-facts box — that's a different panel.)

### Step 2 — Read the allergen line (it flags wheat)

At the *end* of the ingredient list there's a separate, often bolded line for **알레르기 유발물질** (allergens). Korea requires major allergens to be declared here — wheat among them — so if a product contains wheat, it almost always appears on this line as **밀**. That makes it your fastest read for the most common gluten source.

But here's the Korea-specific catch that keeps people from relying on this line alone: **only wheat (밀) is a mandatory allergen. Barley (보리) and rye (호밀) are not.** A clean allergen line rules out *wheat* — not barley or rye. For those, you can't shortcut; you have to read the full ingredient list in Step 3. In Korea that matters, because barley and malt (보리, 엿기름, 맥아, 보리차) turn up constantly.

### Step 3 — Scan the full ingredient list for the gluten words

Don't stop at the allergen line — run your eye up the whole **원재료명** list. Learn to recognize these, and you can clear or reject a product in seconds:

| Hangul | Means | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 밀 | wheat | the most common; on the allergen line |
| 밀가루 | wheat flour | |
| 소맥 | wheat | **same as 밀, different name — the classic trap** |
| 소맥분 | wheat flour | 소맥 family |
| 보리 | barley | malt, 엿기름 (malt syrup), 맥아 (malt), some teas — *not* on the allergen line |
| 대맥 | barley | another name for 보리 (rare on labels) |
| 호밀 | rye | *not* on the allergen line |
| 밀 전분 | wheat starch | **hides in "rice" products** |

The three that catch careful people: **소맥** (a second word for wheat that a "just look for 밀" habit misses), **밀 전분** (wheat starch quietly blended into things sold as rice-based), and **보리** (barley, which won't be on the allergen line at all). The snack that broke me of trusting the allergen line alone was a "rice" cracker: allergen line clean, but 밀 전분 sitting a few items up in the ingredients.

### Step 4 — Check the cross-contamination line

Below the allergens you'll often find a shared-facility warning, phrased like:

> *"이 제품은 밀을 사용한 제품과 **같은 시설에서 제조**"* — made in the same facility as products containing wheat.

For **NCGS**, this is usually a judgment call. For **celiac**, the rule is simpler: treat a shared-facility line — and a bare *"밀 알레르기 유발 가능"* (may contain wheat) — as a reason to skip it, because both signal possible trace gluten the ingredient list won't show. When you need certainty, contacting the manufacturer is the only real answer.

## Three Korea-specific gluten-label traps

- **"쌀로 만든" (made with rice) is not a safety claim.** Rice bread and rice snacks routinely blend in wheat flour or wheat starch. Rice on the front means nothing until the back proves it.
- **"글루텐프리" is self-declared.** Without GFCO or a comparable certification, a front-label gluten-free claim in Korea often means "we didn't add obvious flour" — not "safe for celiac." Verify on the back regardless.
- **"저글루텐 (low-gluten)" is not gluten-free.** For celiac and NCGS alike, low-gluten is still gluten.

## What actually passes (the convenience-store baseline)

Once the flip-and-scan is automatic, you'll find a reliable core of convenience-store items that clear it:

- **Boiled or roasted eggs** (구운/삶은 계란)
- **Whole fruit** — bananas, seasonal fruit
- **Plain yogurt** — check the add-ins
- **Chicken breast packs** — *without* the soy-sauce marinade (soy sauce is wheat; see the [hidden-gluten guide](/blog/hidden-gluten-korean-food))
- **Rice crackers (쌀과자)** — but only *after* you scan, because of the wheat-starch trap above

What fails is most of what you'd grab on autopilot: cup noodles, some triangle-kimbap fillings, most breads and pastries, battered snacks. Full breakdown by brand and flavor in the [convenience-store snack guide](/blog/korean-convenience-store-gf-snacks).

## The two-second habit

It collapses into one reflex: at the shelf, glance at the allergen line for **밀**, then run your eye up the full ingredient list for **소맥 / 보리 / 호밀**, and either put it back or keep it. Early on it's slow and you second-guess yourself. After a few weeks it's a glance — the thing that turns a Korean grocery run from a minefield into a normal errand.

## What to do with this

1. **Memorize four words:** 밀, 소맥, 보리, 호밀 — wheat (both names), barley, and rye. (Barley also hides as 맥아, malt.)
2. **Don't trust the allergen line alone.** It reliably flags wheat, but barley and rye live only in the full ingredient list.
3. **Distrust "rice" and "gluten-free" claims** until the ingredients back them up.
4. **If you're celiac,** treat shared-facility and "may contain wheat" lines as a skip, or ask the manufacturer.
