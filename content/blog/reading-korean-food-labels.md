*Updated August 2026 — added the MFDS citation, romanised the terms, fixed the 엿기름 gloss (malted barley, not malt syrup), and corrected an error: the shared-facility caution line is **required** for designated allergens, not voluntary as an earlier version said.*

Ask me to do one thing in a Korean convenience store and I'll do it without thinking: pick up a new snack, flip it over, and scan the back for one word — **밀** (wheat). It takes about two seconds now. But it took months of getting it wrong to make it automatic, and it's the single most useful habit we built while learning to eat gluten-free in Korea.

Restaurants are a conversation. Packaged food is a *reading test* — and once you can read the label, a huge amount of Korean grocery and convenience-store food opens back up. Here's the exact routine.

## Why Korean labels are their own skill

Two things make Korean packaging different from what you might be used to:

1. **The ingredient list is in Korean, and so are the allergen words.** You can't rely on spotting "wheat" in English — you have to recognize the Hangul.
2. **A front-of-pack claim is the maker's own statement — but "무 글루텐" is not a free-for-all.** Korea does define the term. Under [식품등의 표시·광고에 관한 법률 시행규칙 별표 2](https://www.foodsafetykorea.go.kr/portal/board/boardDetail.do?menu_no=3120&menu_grp=MENU_NEW01&bbs_no=bbs001&ntctxt_no=1091412), a food may be labelled **무(無) 글루텐** only if it either (a) uses no wheat, rye, barley, oats or their crossbreeds and tests at **20mg of gluten per kg or less**, or (b) uses ingredients those grains have had gluten removed from and hits the same 20mg/kg ceiling. That threshold matches the international one, so a compliant 무 글루텐 label is a meaningful claim.

   Two limits sit on top of it, and both matter. The provision says a maker **may** apply the label — it is permitted, not required — and we have found no Korean equivalent of [GFCO](https://gfco.org), a third-party body that audits a facility and licenses a seal. So the number has legal force behind it while the *verification* is the manufacturer's own. And the wording we actually meet most on shelves is **저글루텐** (low-gluten), which is not the defined term: the provision we read defines 무 글루텐 and sets a ceiling for it, and carries no counterpart definition for 저글루텐. Low-gluten is not gluten-free, and unlike 무 글루텐 it isn't anchored to a number. (More on that trap in [the hidden-gluten guide](/blog/hidden-gluten-korean-food).)

Korean law helps — but only halfway. The substances the allergen line must declare are fixed by [식품등의 표시·광고에 관한 법률 시행규칙 별표 2](https://www.foodsafetykorea.go.kr/portal/board/boardDetail.do?menu_no=3120&menu_grp=MENU_NEW01&bbs_no=bbs001&ntctxt_no=1091412) — **소비자 안전을 위한 표시사항** (consumer-safety labelling, under 제5조제1항), which is where the actual list lives. Labelling *format* is then governed by the MFDS standard [식품등의 표시기준](https://www.law.go.kr/행정규칙/식품등의표시기준), the version in force when we read it being [고시 제2025-60호, 29 August 2025](https://www.mfds.go.kr/brd/m_211/view.do?seq=14917) (retrieved August 2026). Both are amended regularly — check them against what's current when you read this.

**밀 (wheat) is on the mandatory list.** The catch that trips up even careful people: **barley (보리) and rye (호밀) are not.** Those you have to hunt for yourself. That distinction is the whole reason this routine has four steps instead of one.

**Where the claims here come from:** the regulatory statements are read off the MFDS standard linked above (retrieved August 2026). Everything about what sits on convenience-store shelves is our own shopping in Seoul — first-hand, but not a survey, and not lab-tested. Where the two disagree, the package in your hand wins.

## The 4-step flip-and-scan for gluten

### Step 1 — Flip it and find the ingredient list

Turn the package over. Look for **원재료명** (*wonjaeryomyeong*, ingredient list) — sometimes printed as **원재료명 및 함량** (ingredients and amounts), usually on the back or the bottom. The ingredient list is one of the items the [식품등의 표시기준](https://www.law.go.kr/행정규칙/식품등의표시기준) requires on packaged processed food, which is why you can count on finding it. (Don't confuse it with 영양성분, *yeongyangseongbun*, the nutrition-facts box — that's a different panel.)

### Step 2 — Read the allergen line

At the *end* of the ingredient list sits a separate box for **알레르기 유발물질** (allergens). The rule requires it to be set off from the background colour, so it's usually the easiest thing on the panel to find. It reads like this — this is the regulation's own worked example:

> 달걀, 우유, 새우, 이산화황, 조개류(굴) 함유

The last word is the one that carries the sentence: **함유** means *contains*. Everything before it is in the product, and the rule is blunt about it — every declared allergen used as an ingredient goes on this line **regardless of how much** is in there.

**Learn the whole line, not just one word.** Most guides tell you to hunt for 밀 and stop. That works until the line is long and you're standing in a 7-Eleven at 11pm, at which point recognising all of it is faster and less error-prone than pattern-matching one character. These are the substances that must be declared, in the order the regulation lists them:

| 한글 | English |
|---|---|
| 알류(가금류) | eggs (poultry) |
| 우유 | milk |
| 메밀 | buckwheat |
| 땅콩 | peanut |
| 대두 | soybean |
| **밀** | **wheat** |
| 고등어 | mackerel |
| 게 | crab |
| 새우 | shrimp |
| 돼지고기 | pork |
| 복숭아 | peach |
| 토마토 | tomato |
| 아황산류 | sulfites |
| 호두 | walnut |
| 닭고기 | chicken |
| 쇠고기 | beef |
| 오징어 | squid |
| 조개류(굴·전복·홍합) | shellfish (oyster, abalone, mussel) |
| 잣 | pine nut |

Nineteen entries. You'll see the count given as 21 or 22 elsewhere, which is the same list with 조개류 split into its three named shellfish — we counted the entries as the regulation writes them.

**Now look at that list for gluten grains. There is exactly one: 밀.** Not 보리 (barley). Not 호밀 (rye). This is the single most important thing on this page, and reading the full list is how you *see* it rather than take our word for it. A clean allergen line establishes one fact — **no declared wheat** — and says nothing whatsoever about barley or rye. For those you cannot shortcut; Step 3 is not optional. In Korea that gap is wide, because barley and malt (보리, 엿기름, 맥아, 보리차) turn up constantly.

One more piece of grammar worth knowing, because it changes what a line means:

| Wording | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **밀 함유** | Wheat is an ingredient. Put it back. |
| **밀 혼입 가능** / **밀 혼입 가능성 있음** | Wheat isn't an ingredient, but the same facility or line handles it. |
| **"…밀을 사용한 제품과 같은 제조 시설에서 제조하고 있습니다"** | The long-form version of the same thing. |

The second and third are the cross-contamination wording covered in Step 4 — and there's a trap in how they interact. The rule says the may-contain notice **is not printed when the substance is already an ingredient**. So a package can carry no 혼입 warning for one of two opposite reasons: nothing to warn about, or it's already declared above. Read the 함유 line first, then the warning — in that order.

### Step 3 — Scan the full ingredient list for the gluten words

Don't stop at the allergen line — run your eye up the whole **원재료명** list. Learn to recognize these, and a package either fails the scan on the spot or moves on to Step 4:

| Hangul | Romanised | Means | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 밀 | mil | wheat | the most common; on the allergen line |
| 밀가루 | milgaru | wheat flour | |
| 소맥 | somaek | wheat | **same as 밀, different name — the classic trap** |
| 소맥분 | somaekbun | wheat flour | 소맥 family |
| 밀 전분 | mil jeonbun | wheat starch | **hides in "rice" products** |
| 보리 | bori | barley | *not* on the allergen line |
| 엿기름 | yeotgireum | malted barley | the malted grain or its powder — 식혜, and used to saccharify syrups |
| 맥아 | maega | malt | barley-derived; turns up in seasonings and drinks |
| 대맥 | daemaek | barley | another name for 보리 (rare on labels) |
| 호밀 | homil | rye | *not* on the allergen line |

One gloss worth getting right, because we had it wrong here until August 2026: 엿기름 is **malted barley**, not malt syrup. The syrups are 물엿 (*mullyeot*) and 조청 (*jocheong*). 물엿 is commonly described as corn-derived and 조청 as traditionally saccharified with 엿기름, but we haven't verified either per product — so treat both as a check rather than a pass, and read the 원재료명.

The three that catch careful people: **소맥** (a second word for wheat that a "just look for 밀" habit misses), **밀 전분** (wheat starch quietly blended into things sold as rice-based), and **보리** (barley, which won't be on the allergen line at all). The snack that broke me of trusting the allergen line alone was a "rice" cracker: allergen line clean, but 밀 전분 sitting a few items up in the ingredients.

### Step 4 — Check the cross-contamination line

Below the allergens you'll often find a shared-facility warning, phrased like:

> *"이 제품은 밀을 사용한 제품과 **같은 시설에서 제조**"* — made in the same facility as products containing wheat.

For **NCGS**, this is usually a judgment call. For **celiac**, the rule is simpler: treat a shared-facility line — or any may-contain wording naming 밀 — as a reason to skip it, because both signal possible trace gluten the ingredient list won't show.

**And here's the part that matters most for celiac.** [식품등의 표시·광고에 관한 법률 시행규칙 별표 2](https://www.foodsafetykorea.go.kr/portal/board/boardDetail.do?menu_no=3120&menu_grp=MENU_NEW01&bbs_no=bbs001&ntctxt_no=1091412) *does* require this caution line where a designated allergen may unavoidably carry over from a shared process — so a printed warning is real information, not boilerplate. But two gaps sit underneath it, and both bite here:

1. **The duty covers designated allergens — and 밀 is one, while 보리 and 호밀 are not.** A facility that also runs barley owes you nothing on this line.
2. **The maker judges what counts as "unavoidable."** That determination happens inside a factory you can't see.

So the asymmetry to carry around is this: a warning that *is* printed tells you something real, and a blank space is much weaker evidence than it looks. Finishing the four steps establishes exactly one thing: **no declared wheat, barley, or rye.** It does not establish that there is no gluten in the package.

## When the label can't answer

Sometimes the four steps end with a package you still can't clear — a shared-facility line, a syrup you can't trace, a seasoning blend listed only as 혼합제제. At that point the label has said everything it can say, and the only move left is to ask the maker. Korean packaging carries a **소비자상담실** (*sobija sangdamsil*, consumer help line) or **고객센터** number, usually printed near the manufacturer's name and address at the bottom of the panel. One sentence, copy-pasteable, covers what the label leaves open:

> **이 제품에 밀, 보리, 호밀이 들어가나요? 밀을 사용하는 제품과 같은 생산라인에서 만들어지나요?**
> *Does this product contain wheat, barley, or rye? Is it made on the same production line as products that use wheat?*

For looking a product up before you're at the shelf, the MFDS runs a public food database at [식품안전나라](https://www.foodsafetykorea.go.kr) (foodsafetykorea.go.kr) — it's Korean-only and not every product is in it, but registered items carry their declared ingredients.

**A standing caution:** Korean manufacturers reformulate without redesigning the package. A snack that cleared the scan last month can fail this month, and the packaging will look identical. Re-scan every time — that's the reason this is a two-second habit and not a memorised list.

## Three Korea-specific gluten-label traps

- **"쌀로 만든" (*ssal-lo mandeun*, made with rice) is not a safety claim.** Rice breads and rice snacks are where we've been caught most often by 밀가루 or 밀 전분 in the ingredients. Rice on the front means nothing until the back backs it up.
- **"글루텐프리" is self-declared.** With no Korean equivalent of a GFCO-style third-party audit that we've been able to find, a front-label gluten-free claim tells you what the manufacturer says, not what an outside body checked. Read the back regardless.
- **"저글루텐 (low-gluten)" is not gluten-free.** For celiac and NCGS alike, low-gluten is still gluten.

## What actually passes (the convenience-store baseline)

Once the flip-and-scan is automatic, a small core of convenience-store items keeps clearing the scan for us. "Clears the scan" means no declared wheat, barley, or rye on the package we read — it is not a safety rating, and every one of these still has to be checked on the day:

- **Boiled or roasted eggs** (구운/삶은 계란)
- **Whole fruit** — bananas, seasonal fruit
- **Plain yogurt** — check the add-ins
- **Chicken breast packs** — *without* the soy-sauce marinade. Brewed Korean soy sauce is a wheat product: [Sempio's own page for 양조간장 501](https://en.sempio.com/product/soysauce/view/598) lists wheat in the ingredients and declares wheat as an allergen (retrieved August 2026). More on the condiments in the [hidden-gluten guide](/blog/hidden-gluten-korean-food)
- **Rice crackers (쌀과자)** — but only *after* you scan, because of the wheat-starch trap above
- **Potato chips — and here the specifics matter.** [Orion's own product page](https://www.orionworld.com/goods/view/27?page=1&goodsno=26&category=0102) lists 포카칩 오리지널 (*Pocachip Original*) with allergens milk, soy and beef, and no 밀 (retrieved August 2026). The **Onion flavour of the same brand declares 밀.** One bag, two flavours, opposite answers — that pair is this entire article in miniature, and it's why the habit is reading the package rather than remembering a brand. Flavour-by-flavour detail is in the [snack guide](/blog/korean-convenience-store-gf-snacks).

What fails is most of what you'd grab on autopilot: cup noodles, some triangle-kimbap fillings, most breads and pastries, battered snacks. Full breakdown by brand and flavor in the [convenience-store snack guide](/blog/korean-convenience-store-gf-snacks).

## The two-second habit

It collapses into one reflex: at the shelf, glance at the allergen line for **밀**, then run your eye up the full ingredient list for **소맥 / 보리 / 호밀**, and either put it back or keep it. Early on it's slow and you second-guess yourself. After a few weeks it's a glance — the thing that turns a Korean grocery run from a minefield into a normal errand.

## What to do with this

1. **Memorize four words:** 밀 (mil), 소맥 (somaek), 보리 (bori), 호밀 (homil) — wheat (both names), barley, and rye. (Barley also hides as 맥아 *maega* and 엿기름 *yeotgireum*.)
2. **Don't trust the allergen line alone.** It reliably flags declared wheat, but barley and rye live only in the full ingredient list.
3. **Distrust "rice" and "gluten-free" claims** until the ingredients back them up.
4. **If you're celiac,** remember what a clean scan proves — no *declared* wheat, barley, or rye — and what it doesn't. A printed shared-facility warning is required and meaningful; a missing one covers neither barley nor rye and rests on the maker's own judgment. Call the 소비자상담실 number on the package when the answer has to be certain.
